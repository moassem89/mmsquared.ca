3 · play it

∀bélard vs ∃loïse

A quantifier sentence is a two-player game. ∀bélard, the challenger, plays every ∀: he picks the value that makes life hardest. ∃loïse, the defender, plays every ∃: she picks a witness, knowing everything chosen so far. The sentence is true exactly when ∃loïse has a winning strategy, and false exactly when ∀bélard has one. Play both sides: each claim below can be won by exactly one of them, and which one tells you whether the claim is true.

Why these names?

They are a logician's pun. The idea that a quantified sentence is a game between two players goes back to the 1950s and 60s (Paul Lorenzen's dialogue games, Jaakko Hintikka's game-theoretical semantics). Hintikka called the players Myself and Nature; other books say Verifier and Falsifier, or Proponent and Opponent. Later writers on game semantics nicknamed them ∀bélard and ∃loïse: the symbol ∀ is an upside-down A and ∃ a backwards E, and the names belong to Peter Abelard (1079–1142), a Paris logician, and Héloïse, the scholar whose letters with him are famous. Nothing mathematical depends on the names. If they get in the way, read them as the challenger and the defender.

4 · write it down

From strategy to proof

In the game you answered one ε at a time. A proof answers all of them at once: it hands ∀bélard a rule, δ as a function of ε, and shows that the rule wins every round. That is all an ε–δ proof is. Three steps: write the rule and let ∀bélard attack it; supply the reasons, line by line; then assemble a proof from scratch. Nothing here is graded by guesswork. A rule fails when a concrete ε and x break it, and the feedback names them.

What is recorded here, and how the live version works

The page cannot read a proof written in free text; only a person or a language model can. In the live version, "Get feedback" sends three things to a model: the claim, a reference proof, and a short list of the mistakes students usually make on it (δ depending on x, the missing cap on δ, a bound used before it is earned, the implication run backwards). The model is asked for feedback, not a grade: what is right, the first gap, the one-line fix, and a question back. A handwritten proof goes the same route after one extra step, a transcription shown to the writer for correction before anything is judged, so misreadings are separated from mistakes.

A static page cannot keep a key secret, so the live version needs either a key the viewer supplies (stored only in their browser) or a small proxy that holds one with a spending cap. This copy ships with neither, on purpose: everything above it works offline, forever, and the two recorded samples show the rest.
